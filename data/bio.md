Thesis
Title: Knowledge Management Determinants of Breakthrough Research Productivity

Core Research Question
To what extent do academic researchers adopt new modes of knowledge productivity — crowdsourcing, open innovation, collaborative digital platforms — and how does this adoption influence research productivity and breakthrough innovation?
The thesis introduced and tested the concept of 'technological propensity': the behavioural orientation describing the degree to which individuals seize opportunities offered by new technologies offering economies of scale (e.g. crowdsourcing, Amazon Mechanical Turk). It examined whether high technological propensity correlates with higher research productivity and what human-level factors moderate this relationship.
Theoretical Background
•	Sixth-generation (6G) innovation models — beyond Rothwell's 5G model; the internet enables crowd-powered, probabilistic innovation
•	Open innovation and open science (Chesbrough); crowdsourcing and collective intelligence
•	Key reference: the Foldit protein-folding experiment — scientists crowdsourced a molecular biology problem stumped researchers for decades; crowd solved it in days
•	Knowledge management theory and problems of knowledge aggregation
•	Callaghan's 6G models combining crowdsourcing and crowdfunding
•	Academic productivity determinants: personality (Big Five), motivational values, work climate perceptions, human capital investments, gender, field of study
Research Design — Three Studies
•	Study 1 (Qualitative): 30 expert interviews with award-winning scientists and innovators worldwide, including 3 Nobel Prize winners, 3 Lasker Award winners, 1 Fields Medal winner. Thematic content analysis to develop a model of 'innovative potential.'
•	Study 2 (Quantitative): Survey of South African academic researchers testing the model. Productivity measured by journal publications.
•	Study 3 (Quantitative): Survey of Amazon Mechanical Turk crowd-workers. Productivity measured by microtask completion rates.
Key Findings
•	Technological propensity positively associated with research productivity — but context-dependent; works best as a complement to certain forms of academic research
•	Prosocial motivations (desire to solve social problems) strongly associated with higher productivity
•	New modes of productivity (crowdsourcing, open platforms) may enhance rates of breakthrough innovation
•	Misaligned incentives in academic contexts can prevent efficient adoption of 6G innovation modes

Asaf Rubin was born on a cold winter's morning in the sprawling metropolis of Johannesburg in South Africa to two confused parents who couldn't for the life of them fathom why this 3kg chunk of flesh would think academia would be a reasonable career path. Private Parts

Subtitle: on credit, cockroaches and cardiology

Cover: the PE CEOs gathered? Or something to do with First Brands / Tricolor 

When a characteristically cheeky Jason Statham finds himself on the wrong side of a gang of Chinese triads in the 2006 cult classic Crank, he wakes up one day to find that a poison that inhibits the flow of adrenaline has been sneakily injected into his veins in the middle of the night, causing his heart rate to slow towards an ultimate cessation of vital function. That is, unless he is able to keep said heart pumping at an abnormally rapid click. On his progressively batshit quest to find and kill the villain that issued his death warrant, Statham bounces around Los Angeles through a series of absolutely ridiculous scenarios to keep the ticker ticking. Anything that’ll keep those adrenaline levels sky-high. A few bumps of cocaine gets the ball rolling, but the effect wears off quickly. Next he’s ghostriding a motorbike through LA traffic. Then he’s holding up hospital attendants at gunpoint to elicit a juiced up defibrillator shock to the chest, then he’s sharing a very public intimate moment with his  girlfriend right in the middle of Chinatown, to the applause of a surprised, then delighted crowd. The essential logic of the film is simple: stop moving and you die. It doesn't matter how insane the movement gets, insanity is the whole point.

The private credit industry has, in the first three months of 2026, been having something of a Crank moment of its own. To understand why requires a brief excursion into the exhilarating somewhat unglamorous history of corporate lending. No Chinese triads in the story unfortunately, but plenty of diabolical plot developments to keep the adrenaline high. 

[Image: Jason Statham in Crank]
[Caption: Artist’s rendition of private credit markets withholding investor withdrawals (Credit: IMDB) 

For most of the post-war era, if it was a loan a a mid-sized company wanted, then the bank was the place to go. The bank evaluated the credit, held the loan on its balance sheet, and charged interest. A boring, slow, and occasionally miserly procedure, but one that was fundamentally stable. But when the world awoke in 2008 to find that a poison was now coursing through its bloodstream, the boring old system that had been so stable for so long suddenly needed a syringe full of adrenaline juice to the heart. And so in 2010, Congress passed the Dodd-Frank Act; a sprawling regulatory overhaul that, among other things, forced banks to hold significantly more capital against risky loans. Suddenly, lending to mid-sized, leveraged, or otherwise complicated companies became considerably less attractive for banks, who duly backed away from the table. And into the gap stepped an assortment of adrenaline junkies alternative asset managers who looked at all that unmet demand for corporate credit and spied themselves an opportunity.

It was the firms that had previously made names for themselves in private equity - Apollo Global Management, Blackstone, Ares Management, KKR and Blue Owl, amongst others - who enthusiastically hopped into the fray, building out massive new credit platforms to do what the banks had just been told to stop doing. To do so, this collection of enterprising firms pooled capital from institutional investors - pension funds, insurance companies, sovereign wealth funds, university endowments - and used it to lend directly to companies. No public markets, no regulatory capital requirements, and crucially - no syndication - that, is, no selling off chunks of your loan to other banks or institutional investors or unsympathetic hedge funds on the secondary market. If Ares or Apollo or any other manner of Greek Deity-cum-financial behemoth sold you a loan, they held the whole thing in their fund until it matures or is repaid. An appealing prospect for borrowers, but with a flip side that when things go wrong, the loss sits entirely with the fund rather than being spread across the system. Nevertheless, in the wake of the 2008 financial crisis, private credit had arrived. And it was exceptionally good at its job.

The numbers since have been, to use the technical term, absolutely mental. From a comparatively skimpy ~$250 billion globally at the time of the financial crisis, private credit grew to $1.7 trillion by the mid-2020s, and is now estimated at somewhere between $2 and $3 trillion depending on who's counting and what they're counting. BlackRock projects the total private debt market will reach $3.5 trillion by the end of 2028. Just ten firms raised over $700 billion in private credit capital in the five years to 2025 alone. Ares Management, the largest, raised $116 billion. Apollo's entire credit business - which spans public and private credit and asset-backed financing - accounts for $749 billion of its $938 billion in total assets under management. “Today, the banks supply less than 20% of all credit to businesses,” mused the ever eloquent Apollo CEO Marc Rowan in a 2023 interview with Fortune. The remaining 80% is increasingly coming from people like him.

[image: chart of capital movement from banks to private lenders post 08]
[Caption: All the manner of adrenaline shots since Dodd Frank arrived on the scene (Credit: Janus Henderson)]

So why did investors pile in so enthusiastically? Once the dust had settled in the wake of the subprime mortgage crisis (and EuroZone crisis that followed), private credit was the nearest thing to a free lunch that sophisticated finance had to offer. Returns averaged around 10.3% per year since 2016, with default rates below 3%. That kind of performance may as well have been a scantily clad and buxom woman on a motorcycle brandishing a juiced-up defibrillator full of cocaine to the Stathamistic investors. And in through the door came barreling a ravenous horde of pension funds with long-dated liabilities and a desperate need for yield, insurance companies with enormous pools of capital looking for something, anything, that paid more than government bonds, family offices, sovereign wealth funds, endowments. The trade worked, the adrenaline flowed, and the heart kept pumping at an abnormally high and progressively cranked-up rate. 

But private credit, as the name implies, is private: the loans are not traded on public markets and there are no real-time prices. Valuations are set quarterly by the managers themselves, using complicated models and esoteric assumptions that lay outsiders cannot easily interrogate. The collateral is often the cash flows of mid-sized companies with no public profile. The whole apparatus sits almost entirely outside of the regulatory perimeter that governs banks. So no Federal Reserve oversight, no deposit insurance, and no mandatory stress tests. “The opaque nature of private credit lenders makes it difficult for regulators to assess risk management practices and the buildup of risks in the sector,” the Financial Stability Oversight Council very diplomatically noted in its most recent annual report. What they meant was that we can't quite see what's happening under the private credit hood, and that makes them nervous as an adrenaline junkie at a book club evening. 

Then again that opacity is a feature, not a bug. Companies like private credit because it’s fast and discreet, and doesn’t require them to disclose sensitive business information publicly. Investors like it because they can lock up capital for three to five years, collect the illiquidity premium - that is the higher yield investors demand holding assets that cannot easily converted into cash - and not worry about mark-to-market volatility. Managers like it because their valuations were - let's say - somewhat self-determined, which means that the portfolios tend to look smooth and stable even when underlying conditions are deteriorating. “Zero loss” became the private credit industry's unofficial tagline, defaults were rare and problems were managed quickly and quietly. 

[image: chart]
[Caption: A cardiologist’s worst nightmare(Credit: Global Legal Insights)

But in late 2025 and early 2026, a handful of things went wrong in rapid succession. It started with First Brands, an auto parts manufacturer - with a not-too-shabby $5 billion in annual sales - which filed for a seemingly out-of-the-blue bankruptcy in late September last year. Its collapse revealed that it had allegedly pledged the same collateral to multiple lenders simultaneously - a practice that is, to put it gently - not considered best form. Then there was Tricolor - a subprime auto lender - which imploded in a similarly spectacular fashion shortly after. JPMorgan’s Jamie Dimon - a man not known for understatement - was the first to reached for the cockroach metaphor: when you find one, he warned, more of the foul invertebrates are bound to be found scurrying about the woodwork nearby. In addition to the near universal revulsion they inspire, the thing about cockroaches is that the little critters are remarkably resilient, having purportedly survived the Chicxulub impact that had the entire dinosaur industry filing for bankruptcy 65 million years ago, and are also likely to survive in the event of an increasingly probable man-made nuclear winter. (Some say they even survived the 2008 financial crash, although those claims are likely exaggerated.) But those cockroaches crawling the arteries of the private credit plumbing have thusfar proven just as hardy as their real-world counterparts. No industry has been safe since the meteoric chunks of the AI revolution have come hurtling out of space, private credit included. A significant portion of private credit portfolios - particularly those originated during the frothy 2020-2022 vintage - was concentrated in enterprise software companies, the exact sector most vulnerable to AI replacing the functions their products perform. Suddenly, the valuations assigned to a lot of loans were looking far too optimistic.

[image: cockroaches]
[Caption: La Cookaracha! (Credit: Finews)

Redemption requests started coming in, and investors who had locked up capital in semi-liquid private credit vehicles - the newer retail-friendly structures designed to make the asset class accessible to a broader audience - began asking for their money back. Not in a panicked, bank-run kind of way, but steadily and insistently, and in volumes that the structures were not designed to absorb. Blue Owl gated withdrawals from a retail credit vehicle in February 2026. Ares Management capped redemptions in its $10.7 billion Strategic Income Fund at 5% after withdrawal requests surged to 11.6%. Apollo marked down assets and cut payouts in one of its business development companies. By late March, more than $4.6 billion in investor capital was sitting behind withdrawal limits across the industry, with more managers expected to follow. The adrenaline that had so vigorously coursed through the system was wearing off and there was nary a defibrillator to steal or girlfriend to help get the blood rushing to the private parts.

Veterans of 2008 have begun making their inevitable comparisons, but it is worth being precise about both why they are being made and why they may be overstated. Sure, the structural echoes are real: opaque assets and self-assessed valuations, ratings that may not accurately reflect underlying risk, a retail investor base that did not fully understand what they were buying, and a market that had expanded far beyond the conditions that justified its original premises. Not to mention a hungry looking Christian Bale carefully eyeing Michael Burry’s every move. In the US, where the crunch has hit hardest, the DOJ and SEC have both opened investigations into valuation practices and ratings integrity in the private credit space - the same kind of conflicts of interest that plagued the mortgage-backed securities market before 2008. Morgan Stanley has warned that default rates in private credit direct lending could surge to 8%, well above the 2-2.5% historical average. These are not trivial concerns.But the differences from 2008 are also material. In our present crisis, the investor base is predominantly institutional - pension funds and sovereign wealth funds who understood they were locking up capital and are not going to pull out in a panic the way retail depositors did from Silicon Valley Bank in 2023. There is also the fact that leverage inside private credit funds is considerably lower than it was in the structured finance vehicles that imploded during the financial crisis. Sub-investment-grade lending, while growing, remains manageable relative to overall GDP. And the practice of ‘gating’, or restricting withdrawals, is by no means a sign of abject collapse. It is, as one analyst put it “the structure doing exactly what it was designed to do.” This is clearly not 2008 Episode II. Or at least, it is not 2008 just yet.

What it is, more precisely, is the first real stress test of a $2 trillion market that has never actually been tested. For fifteen happy years, private credit grew in almost entirely benign conditions: falling interest rates, abundant liquidity, recovering corporate earnings, and an institutional investor base hungry for that sweet yield. The loans written in 2020 and 2021 - the vintage now most in question - were underwritten on the assumption that those conditions would persist. But they have not; interest rates rose, corporate earnings in certain sectors buckled, and the disruption following the AI explosion in 2022 arrived faster than almost anyone could’ve reliably modelled. “We haven't seen a default cycle since 2008,” noted Dan Rasmussen of Verdad Advisers. “This is an untested asset class.” But testing, it seems, has now begun. 

[Image: chart - comparison of 2008 crisis to private credit crisis]
[Caption: A handy comparison, a bad sign for those hoping for a sequel (Credit: Lance Roberts, LinkedIn)

Meanwhile the demons that inhabit regulation hell have begun once again to polish their pitchforks. For years, the private credit industry operated in what regulators called a 'shadow banking' system, which performed bank-like functions without bank-like oversight. And ever since cockroach exterminators Dodd and Frank came bursting through the door in 2010 with their much maligned Act designed to prevent a Statham sequel, several of the largest alternative managers lobbied successfully to avoid being designated 'systemically important financial institutions,' which would have subjected them to Federal Reserve oversight. They argued, not unreasonably, that they were different from banks: no deposit-taking, no leverage at the system level, and no sprawling interconnected payment networks. The argument was technically defensible and the pitchforks have up until now been kept at bay. But the forges of hell always keep an ear out for new opportunities to inflict their torture. The Financial Stability Oversight Council - the demonic entity(strikeout) body created specifically to identify risks that fall between regulatory jurisdictions - has now for quite some time been sounding the alarm about private credit's growing entanglement with insurance companies, its expansion into retail channels, and the fundamental opacity of the whole apparatus. Calls for tighter oversight are growing, and the industry, as is its custom, is already preparing its arguments about why tighter oversight would be counterproductive, even as investors frantically claw at the door to the vault. 

The irony of it all is that private credit exists in its modern form because of regulation. The well-intentioned Dodd-Frank pushed lending out of banks and into the shadows. Those shadows grew big enough to start looking suspiciously bank-like, which in turn catches the all-seeing eyes of the hordes of pitchfork wielding regulators who then wonder whether the risks they displaced from one part of the system have simply reappeared in another. 

There is a Crank-like quality to this dynamic: each new shot of adrenaline creates the conditions for the next. Banks were regulated into retreat; private credit filled the gap. Private credit is now showing signs of stress as regulators consider intervention and that capital will find somewhere else to go. This particular heart keeps pumping, but all hearts - even those worth a couple trillion dollars - eventually stop beating. Those with a bullish disposition argue that this is all merely a healthy repricing, not a collapse. That an 8% default rate (as opposed to the ~3% that held for so long) would be painful in spots but ultimately a healthy reset that frees up capital for stronger businesses. The market will mature, they say, and standards will tighten, the bad vintages will work through the system, and private credit will emerge as a durable, important part of the global financial architecture. They are probably right in the end - the market is too large, too embedded in the financing of too many real businesses, to simply disappear in a puff of smoke. Nearly a trillion dollars in US corporate debt is set to mature between 2026 and 2028, much of it requiring refinancing through private channels. It remains to be seen how it all pans out, but the preliminary diagnosis is that the demand is structural, not cyclical. That being said, “probably fine in the long run” is not quite the same as “nothing to worry about right now,” and the honest answer is that nobody, including the Marc Rowans and Jamie Dimons of the world, knows exactly what is in those portfolios, nor have they ever mapped the complex web of connections between private credit, insurance capital, pension funds and retail investors. The Federal Reserve is the one entity that might ordinarily be expected to step in, assess the situation and provide stability, but they have no formal jurisdiction over most of it.

In the high-octane conclusion to Crank, Jason Statham - in search of the ultimate adrenaline rush to rule them all - leaps from a helicopter mid-air and plummets to what one would logically expect is a gruesome death on the concrete below. Ah! But, as it turns out - and as we discover in the inevitable sequel Crank 2: High Voltage - that the same villains that injected the cheeky Brit with that poison manage to scrape Statham’s body of the pavement, revive him, and have now replaced his organic heart with a mechanical device that requires - wait for it - repeated electric shocks of a progressively higher voltage to keep beating. What began as a workaround for a broken heart - a synthetic replacement for the organic lending function that banks could no longer perform - worked well for a long time. But synthetic hearts require external stimulation to keep beating, the voltage required keeps climbing, and nobody has yet figured out what happens when even that stops working. Crank 2, for what it's worth, ends on another ridiculous cliffhanger. The third film was never made.

A Crowd in the Casino

Subheading: To save the world or sell the sugar water? 

One would be hard pressed to find someone who didn’t fantasise about the possibilities of having a Dustin Hoffman to their own Tom Cruise in Rain Man. A cheat code of your own - a prediction machine - constantly performing inhumanly complex calculations to determine the probability of certain outcomes and telling you exactly where to place the bets that would score you the greatest profit. But there’s something decidedly unsavoury about the whole affair. It gnaws less at our morality than at our intuition about how truth is supposed to be discovered. It’s not only that the poor savant - wielded like a tool - has no say in the matter. Thats bad enough as it is. But its also that the wielder of said tool has desecrated the rules by which we think reality should be weighed.

Online prediction market platform Polymarket made headlines recently for its refusal to reimburse a mystery trader who had placed a large bet on the US ‘invading’ Venezuela. The event did not technically constitute an ‘invasion’, according to Polymarket, and the anonymous gambler - who would have netted a juicy $400,000 - was left empty-handed. The dispute wasn’t really even about geopolitics at all. But it was about contract language. This particular episode of blue balls (strikeout) epididymal hypertension has shed a light on the budding prediction markets industry, which surged from a paltry $100m in monthly trading volume in 2024 to that of a whopping $13b by the end of 2025. 

The pitch is relatively simple; gamblers(strikeout) users of these platforms can place bets(strikeout) trade on the likelihood of future events - all the way from the mundane (sports or the winners of the Oscars or Grammys) to the more the far more exciting realms of politics, markets, crypto, or even space exploration. The categories are essentially limitless. If Vegas is a trip too far or the horse tracks just don’t have that same appeal any more, then Polymarket, Kalshi, PredictIt, Robinhood, et al., have got you covered from the comfort of your own home. Contracts trade continuously, and the idea is that the prices of the bets will inevitably converge towards the crowd’s best estimate of reality, often outperforming traditional polls and even the combined opinions of experts. But the frustrating thing about these incredibly popular new prediction markets is that the contracts only pay out when a predefined condition - often a single legal text - is satisfied. Not necessarily when something turns out to be broadly true. That means these crowds aren’t pricing ‘truth’ per se - they’re pricing the probability that a specific clause will be judged to have been met.

[image - growth of prediction markets industry infographic]
[Caption: xxx]

The idea is nothing new. As far back as 1906, British polymath (and coincidentally, half-cousin to one Charles Darwin) Sir Francis Galton, who is perhaps best known for his coinage of a spate of statistics-students-exasperating terms such as “regression to the mean” and “correlation”, first had the bright idea of inviting the resident villagers of a livestock fair to offer their best estimate of the weight of an ox on display. Nearly 800 participated and the mean of their combined estimate, much to Galton’s surprise, coincided exactly with the actual measurement of the absolute unit of a precisely 1197lb ox. 

“The middlemost estimate expresses the vox populi, every other estimate being condemned as too low or too high by a majority of the voters,” said the brilliant, albeit eugenics-advocating statistician. 

I first became acquainted with the ox-drawn marvels of Galton’s research while pursuing a PhD at The University of Witwatersrand in Johannesburg, under the tutelage of a professor who was fortunately far less prone to bouts of Himmlerian rhetoric, but did deem it fit to dump hard copies of his sprawling research on the obscure practice of ‘crowdsourcing’ on every undergraduate students’ desk before the start of each lecture. The thesis was as follows: in a world constantly teetering on the brink of catastrophe - whether by pandemic, natural disaster or nuclear war - the only effective countermeasure humanity has at its disposal is that of its collective intelligence. Not only the explicit accumulated knowledge archived digitally or on paper, but more importantly that sweet tacit knowledge that resides only in the form of electric currents in people’s brains. And the advent of the internet - and all the information communication technologies that sprouted from it - had extended the reach of those electric currents in people’s brains beyond the limited range of voice alone. The mode of transmission was now global and instantaneous. If innovation is a function of knowledge - that is, the interaction of diverse knowledge inputs coming together to form new ideas - then the internet had essentially unlocked Pandora’s Box.

I took the bait. In fact, I landed up writing an entire PhD thesis about it. 

“Alright, how do monetise?” I asked once I’d submitted the final draft. 

My supervisor was aghast. “This isn’t about selling ‘sugar water’,” was his stern response. “It’s about making a difference! Helping people! Saving the planet!”

Looking back, I don’t think my supervisor was objecting to profit per se, but rather the suspicion that the nature of the knowledge itself changes once money enters the equation. To be fair, that sentiment was indeed the broad consensus way back in the primordial year of 2018. The delightful Jimmy Wales of Wikipedia fame had a similar perspective, spanning back to the crowdsourced encyclopaedia’s founding in 2001. As did the other players in the crowdsourcing world. Examples range from gamified crowdsourcing platform FoldIt, where ‘players’ contributed to crucial advancements in protein folding, and even cancer research, to that of Zooniverse which harnesses crowds to classify galaxies, wildlife, medical images, and historical documents at massive scale. Not to mention Sam Altman murder victim StackExchange, Mecca to the coder community before being ruthlessly superseded by that gregarious chatbot now pinned to your web browser bookmarks bar.

There were however, already some pretences towards the Tom Cruise-style profit motive. The open-innovation and crowdsourcing platform InnoCentive offers a space in which “seekers” (companies) can tap into a global pool of “solvers” (whether experts, amateurs, hobbyists, or anyone with an internet connection, really) to find solutions to difficult problems, in exchange for cash rewards. The best solution wins the prize, and the IP is transferred per the challenge terms. Success stories range from innovative toothpaste tube solutions to oil spill cleanup accelerations. Speaking of cash, it wasn’t long before the realisation dawned that crowds can help accumulate resources other than knowledge. Crowdfunding, on platforms such as KickStarter and GoFundMe, took the world by storm in the late 2000s. 

In the excellent The Wisdom of Crowds, author James Surowiecki argues that large groups can make better decisions than experts - provided that certain conditions are met. With a diversity of opinion, independence and decentralisation, crowds can outperform experts and provide solutions to problems previously considered insoluble. But it’s when those conditions break down that crowds can suddenly turn from wise to stupid. Or even - as per 19th Century Scottish journalist Charles Mackay - stark raving mad. Mackay was the first to document the tendency of crowds to go mad citing examples of witch hunts, the Tulip Mania and other speculative bubbles. All cases where emotion, imitation and authority overpowered reason, where groupthink and herd mentality quickly turned collective wisdom into collective irrationality. Crowds can be wildly - even dangerously - wrong, especially when fear, greed, or belief spreads unchecked. 

The literature on stigmergy describes how the individual agents in flocks of birds that fly in perfect formations, swarms of bees that build hives and angry neckbeards review bombing the penultimate episode of Stranger Things are able to work together towards achieving a common goal, despite a complete lack of centralised coordination. Adam Smith lifts an invisible middle finger in economist heaven. And therein lies the question - Mr. PhD in crowdsourcing: why do crowds act so dumb if they are supposedly so smart? How can crowds do complicated cancer research one minute then turn around the next and collectively decide to start hoarding toilet paper? How can crowds produce astonishingly accurate knowledge one moment, then collectively distort it the next?

The findings of my research pointed to what any half-way decent economist will screech at you from the rooftops: incentives shape behaviour - and behaviour shapes what counts as knowledge. And the trick - if you wish to extract as much value as you can from the stigmergical - is to align those incentives in a way that suits your purpose, just as Cruise coerced Hoffman to the Blackjack table. 

And this is the tension the likes of Polymarket, Kalshi, PredictIt, et al., have so successfully exploited. Crowds are pretty damn good at predicting things, but those predictions can easily slip into insanity and chaos if the incentives aren’t aligned. A little motivation however, goes a long way. Better if there’s a little something in it for them. This is why the likes of InnoCentive and Amazon’s Mechanical Turk work so well, and why people have always flocked to the racetracks, casinos and lotteries. What Polymarket accidentally demonstrated with its latest Venezuelan ‘technicality’ is that the mechanism works exactly as designed. Polymarket was asking whether the specific conditions written into the contract had been satisfied - not necessarily whether or not Venezuela has been invaded in some intuitive or geopolitical sense. Crowds don’t give a decentralised ass about what feels true or honourable, but they do respond to incentives. And so wiring money directly into belief turns the truth-seeking process into something that can be arbitraged. In this particular case, the crowd duly priced the risk of adjudication, as opposed to actual military risk. 

When people can make money from what they think will happen, they stop trying to figure out what’s actually true and instead focus on how everyone else will interpret a rule, a headline, or a technicality. That can be extraordinarily useful when you want to forecast elections or interest rates or even wars. But much to the dismay of my supervisor and the Jimmy Wales of the world, it also means quietly turning the collective intelligence of humanity into a financial instrument - one that rewards second-order belief over first-order truth. The ox still gets weighed, but its mass is only of secondary importance to the estimate of how everyone else will interpret the scale. 

The temptation to find shortcuts in our modern world of instant gratification is entirely understandable. Who wouldn’t want their own little pocket Dustin Hoffman whispering the odds in their ear before the wheel stops spinning? Prediction markets are finely tuned machines that aggregate belief under incentive. Sometimes that produces wisdom of staggering precision, and sometimes it produces madness dressed up as consensus. Crowds can weigh an ox and forecast an election, but wiring money into the circuit changes the question from “what is true?” to “what will settle?” And in that subtle shift from truth to payout lies both the save-the-world wisdom and sugar-water madness of crowds. 

China, Crichton & Chaos

Subheading: Life, uh… finds a way

Across Michael Crichton’s extensive corpus of novels and screenplays, the prolific author explored how bad incentives could turn even the most promising new technologies into abject horror. Published in 1992, Jurassic Park is broadly considered Crichton’s opus; the tale of a eccentric billionaire who decides his money is best splurged on building a theme park on a remote Central American island, full to the brim with prehistoric beasts that had roamed the world 65 million odd years prior. The science is sound and impressive; the geneticists are able to extract and recombine dinosaurian DNA and bring the magnificent creatures back to life. But that 65 million year gap proves a little too large. Not only are the billionaire’s incentives misaligned, but so are those of the previously extinct animals and the human scientists, investors and tasty looking lawyers that naively think they’re in control of the zoo. 

[image - Jurassic Park]

In February of 2025, I, along with 20-odd fellow students had the privilege of partaking in the INSEAD MBA program’s optimistically titled Building a Business in China; a five day trek across the the world’s second most-populous country, from the breathtaking Bund of Shanghai to the sober squares of Beijing, and the physics-defying (350kph!) bullet train between the two. The itinerary our INSEAD / Tshingua professor crafts for us is comprehensive; from financial services to logistics, robotics to AI to tech giants (I unfortunately miss out on the visit to the animation studio, owing to a night of excessive karaoke, Maotai and 3:00am dumplings the night prior). 

To the Westerner arriving on these shores imbued with rare earths, the People’s Republic seems one of a bizarre theme park of its own; Chinese firms are extraordinary and magnificent beasts that move with a speed and coordination that would cause regulatory aneurysms in Europe. But they operate within a strict set of electrified-fence-like boundaries carefully set and maintained by an omnipresent Chinese government which strategically selects industries to prioritise, research to fund, companies to subsidise and even to go as far as to influence career trajectories of the country’s best talent. 

The most striking difference however, is cultural; rugged individualism, heroic founder mythologies and all the other wonderfully Western ideals we take for granted are notably absent here. Or at least, not easily detectable on this surface level. Instead, the businesses we visit - and the employees of those business to whom we speak - trumpet a consistent and proud emphasis on social values, collectivism and contributions to the greater good. It’s an admirable stance, but I am somewhat sceptical, perhaps cynically so. There is something to be said about human nature here, after all. Not to mention the great and obvious Orwellian government-shaped eye firmly lodged into the horizon. Velociraptors are all very well-behaved while the electric fences still work.

Perhaps not the most impressive, but the most telling visit was that of our trip to the head offices of Zhipu AI, in Beijing. In preparation, the lot of us all downloaded the then-recent Chinese generative AI miracle story DeepSeek which had just caused so much chaos upon its release. DeepSeek is good. It feels a little less smooth around the edges than a ChatGPT or a Claude or Perplexity, but the fundamentals are there. We decided to test its limits. 

[Screenshot of Deepseek convo - “What happened to Jack Ma?”]

The Zhipu AI folk were a friendly, excitable bunch. We decided to test their limits. “How do you plan on competing with DeepSeek?” 

"We don’t compete,” came the carefully rehearsed response. “We are working towards the same goal." 

A PR spin it may have been, but perhaps the answer reflected a deeper cultural and economic reality. Perhaps these careful and well-prepared Zhipu employees’ goals really are perfectly aligned with national priorities. We Westerners tend to think of competition as a zero sum game, but from the Chinese perspective, companies are expected to borrow, iterate, and improve upon each other’s successes, rather than fight for dominance. But is that the true nature of companies and the people that inhabit them? There’s no possible way to know for sure. But the same employees squirm when we ask about the company’s intention to IPO. A manager sitting in the back stands up to quickly change the subject. 

Perhaps contemplating the ‘nature’ of companies is anachronistic in this context. Is there something to be said of ‘nurture’ here? The entire approach to innovation is rooted in a different philosophy, where imitation isn’t just accepted, but actively encouraged. The flagship item on our itinerary is that to a Xiaomi factory in Beijing, China’s answer to Apple, Tesla and Sony all at once. The T-Rex of the park, if you will. Xiaomi sets a strict profit margin cap of a razor-thin 5%, prioritising market share and user loyalty over short-term profitability allowing it to reach a massive customer base; products are designed to be affordable and widely adopted. Unlike Western companies and their customers obsessed with privacy, Xiaomi and its users are comfortable trading data for convenience. And unlike Western firms that typically focus on a single vertical, Xiaomi builds interconnected ecosystems far beyond smartphones, expanding into IoT, smart home products, and now even EVs. Only possible, of course, in a context of this very specific regulatory environment.

We tour the almost-entirely automated Xiaomi EV factory floor, on the condition of a strict policy of “no videos, no pictures”, which of course our bellicose resident Italian student promptly violates from the back of the little buggy that wends through a clinical and labyrinthine maze of sci-fi-esque giant robotic limbs diligently hammering and lifting and welding. Little Autonomous Mobile Robots (AMRs) scurry along the factory floor, intelligently dodging our buggy, beeping and booping and transporting materials back and forth like little WALL-E wannabes. The most striking feature is the complete lack of humans. A white lab-coated engineer will occasionally stick their head out, then disappear back into their offices. It’s a scene out of a Crichton prelude. The buggy stops and two heavily armed security men rush at us to grab the Italian’s phone and delete the illegal footage. He keeps his mouth shut, for once. 

[image - Xiaomi factory] 

Whether the mining and robotics company Boonray in Shanghai, or the pristine and impressive showroom floor of Nio (we take one of their EVs for a spin through Pekinese traffic), or the unimaginative but aptly named ‘Best Services International Freight’, the theme is consistent. Handshakes and smiles, proud proclamations of collaboration across the industry, perfect alignment with government objectives, but a nervous twitch and a quick change of subject when we get too inquisitive. 

What took Jurassic Park - and for that matter, his entire oeuvre - beyond just another summer blockbuster, was Crichton’s fascination with Chaos Theory, which purports that complex systems - weather conditions, stock markets, prehistoric theme parks - are predictable in the short term, but impossible to forecast as time goes on and as tiny inaccuracies compound and  exponentially grow. They are inherently deterministic, and ultimately chaotic - think the famous example of the butterfly flapping its wings ultimately resulting in a tsunami half-way across the world. This is why the batshit insane conception of a theme park full reincarnated dinosaurs on a remote island was bound to fail. The Chinese national economy is a - perhaps the - textbook definition of a complex system. That doesn’t necessarily mean its destined to fail, as the Peter Zeihans of this world will have you believe, but if the collapse of the Soviet Union taught us anything its that it wasn’t one singular major event that led to the collapse, but rather the compounding of those tiny imperfections over time. 

To our untrained eyes, the park seems in good shape. The creatures are well fed and roam free (albeit, within their confines of their enclosures). What we as guests don’t see however, are the incredibly complex interventions that prevent nature from taking its course. The beasts are not free to fight, hunt and eat one another, nor breed to their heart’s desire. Darwin rolls in his grave as survival of the fittest becomes survival of the ass-kisser, Crichton smirks in his grave as the tiny imperfections inevitably pile up. The rest of us marvel at the spectacle of it all, the thrill of the fact that life… uh…. finds a way.

The Ozempic Games

[cover image: injections]

Subheading: Fat Suits, Food Noise and Fine Print Fantasies

The obesity rate amongst adults in the United States was 23.2% when Eddie Murphy donned the fat suit in the 1996 remake of The Nutty Professor. That was a pretty substantial increase from 33 years prior when the lesser-known original film - in which the eponymous professor sought a cure for his awkwardness rather than his obesity - was released, and when the US obesity rate was a comparatively svelte 13.4%. Perhaps thats why the WHO recognised obesity as a global epidemic the following year in 1997. By the time the sequel - The Klumps - waddled into cinemas in 2000, the US adult rate of obesity had ballooned to a whopping 30.3%. Today that figure hovers around 40%. 

But the Eddie Murphy films weren’t such box office smash hits because of their statistical significance. Murphy’s a funny guy, sure. But there was also something oddly endearing about watching the jolly Professor Sherman Klump shuffle across the screen. We watched the character out of of fascination and pity, and despite its camp - we followed the plot with that little excited itch in the back of the same parts of our minds that activates late at night when we’re fantasising about the impossible. What if all our problems could be solved with a simple swig of a potion or the pop of a pill? The oft-cited 2011 Bradley Cooper thriller Limitless featured a similar plot-device that so enthralled the audience; a simple pill that imbues its swallower with the ability to utilise 100% of their brain capacity. There was always a catch though. Both the slimming serum and the genius pill turned out to have some pretty nasty side-effects, and our heroes must decide if the juice is worth is the squeeze. Fantasies always have fine prints. For our fat professor, slimness comes with an evil alter ego. For the slacker with the Limitless pill, productivity comes with dangerous blackouts. It’s the same question the likes of Barry Bonds, Lance Armstrong and legions of scrawny gym-goers ask themselves when they weigh up their wildest dreams and risk tolerance. 

[image: nutty professor / sherman klump & buddy love]
[caption: results may vary (Credit: Science Fiction Station)]

The history of weight loss is a carnival of desperation. Tapeworm pills sold in the early 1900s, amphetamine-based diet drugs prescribed with cheerful abandon through the 1950s and 60s, the catastrophic fen-phen debacle of the 1990s which caused heart valve damage in users and led to one of the largest pharmaceutical settlements in history. The fat suit has always come off eventually, but the price has usually been steep and often grotesque. And so the fantasy endured precisely because reality kept delivering such grim alternatives. Klump's magic potion, for all its narrative problems, was at least honest about its nature. It was obviously too good to be true. That was the whole point. 

But the lines between reality and fantasy were forever blurred in 2021 with the FDA’s approval of semaglutide for chronic weight management. The timing couldn’t have been better. Your year-long COVID lockdown binge now had a nifty little ‘undo’ button hovering at the top of the page. All you had to do was click. The approval triggered a 587% increase in prescriptions of the miracle compound in the period between 2021 and 2024.

The best thing about semaglutide is its origin story. Semaglutide is one of a class of drugs called ‘GLP-1 receptor agonists’. GLP-1 is a naturally-occurring hormone your gut produces after you eat that tells your brain you’re full, slows gastric emptying and regulates blood sugar. Essentially, it tells your brain when to stop stuffing your face and go do something else with your life. But as we all know when we shove our heads in the fridge/pantry for that midnight snack, or drive past McDonalds after a long day’s work, the GLP-1 you secrete in your belly is fickle, and therefore unusable as a drug. Luckily for us, there was in the 1990s, an unlikely collision of the fields of endocrinology and herpetology, when a gang of interdisciplinarily-curious researchers found that the saliva of a venomous lizard native to the American Southwest - the ‘Gila monster’ - contained within it a compound that caused the pancreases of victims of its bite to swell - this apparently suggesting an overstimulation of insulin-producing cells. “I know you’ve just been attacked by a demonic reptile, but let me examine your pancreas,” was likely the conversation starter. That compound in the Gila monster’s saliva - Extendin-4 - mimicked GLP-1 but crucially, was resistant to degradation. This is why obesity is so low among the Gila monster community, and why semaglutide - the synthetic version of Extendin-4 - was first developed for type 2 diabetes management, approved for that purpose in 2017 under the brand name Ozempic. 

[image: chart - GLP1 sales / proliferation]
[caption: projected growth of Gila monster saliva sales (Credit: Roots Analysis] 

Weight loss was initially an unintended but welcome side effect. Further developments produced Wegovy - a higher-dose version specifically approved for obesity - and then tirzepatide (commonly known as Mounjaro). In contrast to the sketchy chemical weight-loss solutions of the past, semaglutide has thus far proven to have little to no adverse side-effects. A little gastric distress here and there, maybe a little muscle wastage - nothing that can’t be fixed with some ginger tea, a protein shake and a gym membership. But no evil Klumpian alter egos, no dangerous Limitless blackouts. Have we finally found the magic potion after all? 

As the word spread, the stocks soared, the prices plummeted, and the availability proliferated. Everyone knows someone on the stuff. I’ve never tried it myself, but my curiosity has reached a fever pitch. Users consistently describe a silencing of what has come to be called 'food noise' - that constant low-level cognitive hum of intrusive thoughts about what to eat next and when, the background radiation of craving that pulses through a certain kind of mind regardless of how recently or adequately it has been fed. Food noise is a multi-platinum smash hit in the soundtrack of my own brain, from the days of being an obese teenager who weighed in at an egregious 110kgs at the ripe old age of 14, a dangerously rapid descent to 65kgs at age 17, and who has since fought a constant uphill battle to tame that demonic squawking in my skull. It’s been over 20 years since I was obese and still I’ll often find myself wracked with unquenchable lust for dough and cheese and oil and sugar, regardless of if I’m hungry or not. It’s by sheer willpower alone that I am - mostly - able to overcome the urges to fridge-surf off into Klumptopia. Semaglutide, for many users, turns that noise off. Not the hunger itself, but the obsession - the compulsive loop. Which is why early research showing efficacy for the treatment of alcohol dependence and nicotine addiction is not actually all that surprising. The drug appears to be doing something deeper than suppressing appetite - it may be modulating the reward circuitry that underlies craving itself.

At a value of $45.3b in 2025 - and expected to reach $122.3b by 2030 - the business of food noise silencing is enormous. And it’s rapidly packing on the pounds. The GLP-1 market is currently dominated by two major players: Novo Nordisk, the Danish pharmaceutical giant whose Ozempic and Wegovy have turned it into the most valuable company in Europe (briefly surpassing the GDP of Denmark itself), and Eli Lilly, the American incumbent whose tirzepatide is mounting a serious challenge. Behind them is a scrambling peloton of pharmaceutical companies racing to develop oral formulations. That is because weekly injections, however effective, are a significant barrier to mass adoption, and the prize for a daily pill to mute your munchies demons is essentially incalculable. 

The race is genuinely global. In China, where a formerly impoverished population has suddenly discovered the joys of refined carbohydrates, rates of obesity have skyrocketed. As per the Chinese measurement of BMI (which differs from that used by the WHO), over 50% of the Chinese adult population is either overweight or obese. By sheer numbers, the country has the largest diabetic population in the world, totalling 147 million people. Domestic Chinese pharma companies are developing their own GLP-1 variants at pace. As of 2025, more than 60 Chinese GLP-1 drugs were in development, with over 20 companies developing generics to compete with Western brands. The market is projected to swell to 40 billion yuan ($5.5 billion) by 2030 as generic versions become available post-patent expiry in 2026. 

[image: heatmap of world’s most obese countries]
[caption: the world, mapped by Klumpiness (Credit: WorldAtlas ] 

Similarly in India - long stereotyped as a nation of willowy vegetarians - an estimated 254 million adults are now classified as obese, and nearly 90 million live with type 2 diabetes. Mounjaro became India's top-selling drug by value last year, but at 15,000 - 22,000 rupees a month (~$200), it remained entirely out of reach for the vast majority of people who needed it most. The expiration of Novo Nordisk’s semaglutide patent just last week opened the floodgates for over 40 local generic manufacturers to launch their copycat formulations, with some slashing starter doses to as little as 1,290 rupees ($14) per month. It bears remembering that India is known as the world's pharmacy - its generic drug industry supplies roughly 20% of global off-patent medicines. By the end of 2026, semaglutide patents will have expired in ten countries representing 48% of the global obesity burden - Brazil, China, South Africa, Turkey, Canada among them - and Indian manufacturers are already lining up to supply them all. The magic pill is about to become considerably more magical for considerably more people.

The downstream economic consequences of all this are, depending on your perspective, either thrilling or terrifying. For the reigning monarchs of the multi-billion dollar Ultra-Highly Processed Food industry, valued globally at just under $1 trillion (the US alone accounts for almost a third), its a nightmare of Ronald McDonald proportions. The beloved Golden-Arched themselves reported a dip in same-store sales and have begun to quietly reformulate portion sizes. Research suggests that GLP-1 users spend less on both fast food and groceries. Meanwhile protein demand has soared, as a result of the muscle wasting side-effects that tends to afflict said users. On the other side of the aisle is the weight loss industry - a $70 billion ecosystem of meal replacement shakes, slimming clubs, gym memberships and motivational retreats, facing an existential reckoning. Insulin manufacturers, bariatric surgeons, dialysis providers and orthopaedic implant companies are all are quietly updating their long-term forecasts. Obesity costs the US healthcare system an estimated $173 billion a year. If that number declines materially, it represents one of the largest transfers of economic value in the history of medicine. The money currently absorbed by the consequences of obesity by way of hospital stays, medications, surgeries, lost productivity, has to go somewhere. Or more precisely, it stops needing to go anywhere. That is genuinely and straightforwardly a good thing. But it is also, for a significant number of people whose business models depend on the status quo, catastrophic.

[image: fast food share price dip]
[Caption: would you like extra fries with that? (Credit: Philpp Dubach] 

There have always been sketchy profit motives lurking in the outer boroughs of the healthcare and pharmaceutical industries. The cynical read of the GLP-1 revolution is that it replaces one form of pharmaceutical dependency - insulin, blood pressure medications, diabetes drugs - with anothechoicer, arguably more profitable one. Novo Nordisk's Wegovy costs around $1,300 a month in the United States without insurance. Meanwhile, the same drug costs only fraction of that - between $90 - $350 per month - in Europe. The drug works, but in its current pricing configuration it works considerably better if you're rich. Which is why the first beneficiaries of the GLP-1 revolution were, almost without exception, wealthy. In the United States - and increasingly elsewhere - obesity is disproportionally a condition of poverty. The ‘Ozempic face’ phenomenon that surged through Hollywood in the years after COVID has yet to have arrived in the urban projects, trailer parks and rural food deserts that dot the US. The drug that promised to democratise weight loss initially served to make thinness even more efficiently a signifier of wealth than it already was, and the people for whom the drug would make the most difference are precisely those for whom $1,300 a month is not a serious option.That has already begin to change as patents expire, generics emerge, production costs fall and insurance coverage expands.The trajectory of most transformative drugs almost always tends from unaffordable miracle to routine prescription. There's no reason to think GLP-1s will be different. But 'eventually' is not a satisfying answer when the gap between the people who can afford the pill now and the people who can't maps so neatly onto existing inequalities of race and class. And the access question becomes considerably more pointed when one extends it to what comes next.

In 2018, Chinese scientist Jiankui He casually announced the birth of gene-edited babies who’d been altered with CRISPR - the gene-editing technology that allows those who can afford to wield it the ability to precisely reconfigure their DNA into ways that can prevent disease. Theoretically these “molecular scissors” would also enable cosmetic changes, physical and mental performance upgrades and be used to slow the aging process, a la Bryan Johnson. The complexity of such seemingly far-fetched theoretical interventions remains a feat too difficult, expensive and morally repugnant to have actually been implemented in full-grown humans just yet. Human embryos however - given their relative genetic simplicity - make for a tempting sandbox in which to play God. Designer babies are - if you have the financial and moral aptitude for it - only an ethics-deficient Chinese scientist away. The response in 2018, was at first then shock, then outrage. That hasn’t stopped the likes of the aforementioned Johnson and his tech-bro ilk from exploring whats in it for them. For the low price of $25,000, you can catch a flight to Próspera - a bespoke special economic zone on the coast of Honduras with its own conveniently permissive regulatory framework - and receive a follistatin gene therapy from Minicircle, a startup that promises to increase your muscle mass and possibly extend your lifespan, backed by approximately the same volume of peer-reviewed evidence as a Reddit thread. If Honduras feels too adventurous, Clinique La Prairie on the shores of Lake Geneva offers a week-long programme featuring DNA testing, neurocognitive mapping, transcranial stimulation and cryotherapy for the more discerning immortality-seeker.

[Caption: From Sherman Klump to Buddy Love (Credit: NDTV)

Who gets to take the magic pill? And when the magic pill does more than cure disease - when it begins to augment, to optimise, to enhance - who decides what enhancements are acceptable, and for whom? These are not just hypothetical questions with the comfortable buffer of decades before they need answering. They are questions that the current generation of GLP-1 drugs have already started asking, and which the next generation will ask more loudly. The history of enhancement is a history of access determining outcome: steroids in professional sport, Adderall in elite universities, cosmetic surgery in entertainment, private healthcare in ostensibly public systems. The pattern is consistent; the enhancement arrives, the wealthy adopt it first, and the debate about whether it's acceptable happens mainly among people who can already afford it. By the time it becomes widely available, the early adopters have moved on to the next one.

In the Hollywood takes on magic pills and potions, the fine print is always legible. Take the drug and you risk - in the case of Klump - becoming an asshole. Or in the case of Cooper, blacking out and waking up in the middle of a shootout with angry Chechnyan hitmen. But what makes the GLP-1 moment so interesting is that the fine print is, for once, remarkably small and safe to ignore. The drug works, and the side effects are manageable. That replaces the difficult question the scrawny gym rat would have to ponder when contemplating if an extra plate on the bench press was worth a life of shrivelled gonads and a horse-shoe hair style. Now that the pill actually works without much downside, then what exactly is the objection? That choice then becomes one with societal-level consequences the likes of which no one - not even Eddie Murphy - has had the foresight to ponder. The Nutty Professor just wanted to dance at his own wedding without sweating through his tuxedo. A modest ambition, in retrospect. We've come some distance since then. The Gila monsters, one imagines, are thrilled.
